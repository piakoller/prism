	•	"Reasonably concordant" allows clinically justified extrapolation (e.g., pancreatic NET G2 evidence may inform pancreatic NET G1-G2 treatment decisions, midgut NET evidence may apply to small bowel NET with similar characteristics).

	2.	**Treatment Context / Clinical Applicability** (should be applicable in at least one clinical scenario)
The study's treatment approach, setting, and patient population should provide meaningful evidence for at least one relevant clinical scenario for this patient. Consider:
	•	**Current state:** Patient's actual clinical situation
	•	**Alternative scenarios:** e.g. What if surgery is/isn't feasible? What if SSTR-PET is positive? Different treatment sequences?
	•	**Treatment approach:** Is the studied intervention something that could be considered for this patient?
	•	**Eligibility context:** While strict eligibility criteria shouldn't be a hard rejection, consider if the study population is reasonably similar to this patient.
	•	**Evidence transferability:** Can the treatment insights from this study population be reasonably applied to this patient's situation?

	3.	**Evidence Quality and Relevance** (should provide actionable clinical insights)
The published results should offer meaningful evidence that could guide treatment decisions:
	•	Study provides clear efficacy and/or safety data
	•	Results are clinically meaningful (not just statistical significance)
	•	Patient population studied is representative enough to inform decisions for this patient
	•	**Eligibility considerations:** While the patient may not meet all inclusion criteria, the study population should be sufficiently similar that treatment insights are transferable
	•	Evidence strength justifies consideration in treatment planning

**IMPORTANT:** Publication quality is not a standalone criterion. Focus on whether the prospective clinical evidence (even from preliminary results or abstracts) provides meaningful insights for treatment decisions.

Clinical Assessment:
**1. Patient Summary and Disease Characteristics**
The patient is a 70-year-old male with a heavily pre-treated, metastatic, non-functioning pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor (NF-Pan-NET). The tumor biology is well-differentiated, Grade 2, with a Ki-67 index of 9%. The disease is currently characterized by progressive, bilobar hepatic metastases with a significant tumor burden (largest lesion 70mm in segment IV), following a surgical resection of the primary tumor in 2016. **2. Treatment History and Response Analysis**
The patient has undergone seven lines of systemic therapy, indicating a refractory disease course:
*   **Somatostatin Analogues (SSA):** Progression on Lanreotide (1st line).
*   **PRRT (Peptide Receptor Radionuclide Therapy):** The patient received 4 cycles ending in March 2021. The best response was Stable Disease (SD). According to ENETS 2023 guidelines (Section Q5), retreatment with PRRT may be considered in patients who previously responded (including stabilization) and have a progression-free interval exceeding one year. The interval from March 2021 to the current progression exceeds this threshold.
*   **Targeted Therapies:** The patient demonstrated primary resistance to Everolimus (Best response: Progressive Disease) and Belzutifan (Best response: Progressive Disease). Sunitinib (7th line) was discontinued due to cumulative toxicity (asthenia, G2 thrombocytopenia) despite the patient recovering to fragility status after one month.
*   **Chemotherapy:** The patient achieved Stable Disease/minor response with Streptozocin-5FU (5th line) and Stable Disease with CAPOX (6th line). It is noted that while the patient received Capecitabine (within CAPOX) and Streptozocin (alkylating agent), the specific regimen of Temozolomide alone or in combination with Capecitabine (CAPTEM)—highlighted in ENETS Q7 and ESMO guidelines as a standard option—has not been explicitly documented in the history.

**3. Clinical Assessment of Late-Line Options and Limitations**
Based on the ENETS 2023 and ESMO 2020 guidelines, the following clinical factors are critical for assessing late-line options:

*   **PRRT Retreatment Assessment:**
    *   **Guideline Support:** ENETS guidelines (Section Q5) and ESMO guidelines support considering PRRT retreatment (Re-PRRT) in selected patients with prior response/stabilization and sufficient time since the last cycle.
    *   **Bone Marrow Safety:** The patient presents with persistent G1 thrombocytopenia (recovering from G2). ESMO guidelines warn that up to 3-4% of patients may develop irreversible bone marrow toxicity. The persistent thrombocytopenia requires careful evaluation of bone marrow reserve before considering Re-PRRT or cytotoxic chemotherapy.
    *   **Imaging Requirement:** Eligibility for PRRT retreatment strictly requires confirmation via Somatostatin Receptor Imaging (SRI), preferably PET/CT. As per standard nuclear medicine criteria, uptake in all metastatic liver lesions must exceed normal liver background activity. The current SRI status is not provided in the patient information.

*   **Chemotherapy Assessment:**
    *   **Guideline Support:** ENETS Q7 and ESMO guidelines identify Temozolomide-based regimens (e.g., CAPTEM) as active in Pan-NETs. While the patient has received alkylating agents (Streptozocin) and Capecitabine (in CAPOX), Temozolomide specifically has not been used.
    *   **Toxicity Barrier:** The cumulative toxicity from Sunitinib and persistent G1 thrombocytopenia significantly impact the risk-benefit ratio for further myelosuppressive chemotherapy (such as Temozolomide).

*   **Targeted Therapy Assessment:**
    *   Re-challenge with TKIs or mTOR inhibitors is not supported by the guidelines given the prior progression on Everolimus and intolerance to Sunitinib.

**4. Conclusion**
The patient presents with progressive, liver-dominant metastatic disease after exhausting standard lines of therapy. The clinical assessment points toward two potential guideline-based avenues for evaluation, heavily constrained by the patient's fragility and hematological toxicity:
1.  **PRRT Retreatment:** Feasible based on the duration of response to the first course (>1 year since March 2021), contingent upon adequate bone marrow function and confirmed high uptake on updated SRI.
2.  **Temozolomide-based chemotherapy:** A potential systemic option per ENETS Q7, though the patient's persistent thrombocytopenia and fragility may limit tolerability.

	•	Summary: In gastro-entero-pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (GEP-NETs), radical surgery provides good long-term outcome and low recurrence rates. In GEP-NETs the actual surgical planning is established on the ground of preoperative morphology images (CT scan), and functional imaging using CT/PET with 68Ga-DOTA-TOC, since the high expression of somatostatin receptors (SSR) of these tumors. RGS in GEP-NETs, mainly with gamma-probes, has been not widely accepted since the low rates of sensitivity and, in particular, specificity, in discriminating tumoral/ non tumoral tissue and background ratio. This is a relevant issue in particular in detecting metastatic lymph-nodes both for small-intestine neuroendocrine tumors (SI-NETs) and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (Pan-NETs), where the presence of lymph-node metastases has been associated with worse long-term outcome. At present, it is not possible to distinguish whether a small lymph-node is site of metastases or not without performing frozen sections. In a previous study ex-vivo from European Institute of Oncology SI-NET presented a high uptake of a beta-emitting radiotracer, 90Y-DOTA-TOC. Five SI-NET showing SSR positivity at PET with 68Ga DOTA-TOC received 5 mCi of 90Y-DOTA-TOC the day before surgery. All the tumor samples showed high counts of radioactivity with a sensitivity of 96% and a specificity of 100%. These results allowed the investigators to develop a probe, which is now approved for in-vivo employment within the operating theatre. The objective of the present study is to verify in-vivo within the abdominal cavity the capability of the probe to detect 68-Ga activity within tumoral tissue thus favouring radical surgery and avoiding unnecessary demolition, in the near future. However, in the present protocol the entity of surgery will not be modified by intraoperative findings of the probe. It is reasonable to assume that results from 68Ga-DOTA-TOC might be comparable to 90Y-DOTA-TOC as radiotracer, and the detection efficacy of the probe for 68Ga could be not inferior compared to the isotope 90Y. However, while 90Y-DOTA-TOC is used as investigational drug for therapy purposes only within clinical research protocol, 68Ga-DOTA-TOC is a diagnostic radiotracer broadly used in day-to-day clinical practice since many years. Furthermore, the administration of 68Ga-DOTA-TOC can be directly injected in surgery room and thus does not require patients' admission the day before surgery.

PubMed Full Content: Full text links Cite Display options Display options Format Abstract PubMed PMID Abstract Background: Radio-guided surgery (RGS) holds promise for improving surgical outcomes in neuroendocrine tumors (NETs). Previous studies showed low specificity (SP) using γ-probes to detect radiation emitted by radio-labeled somatostatin analogs. Objective: We aimed to assess the sensitivity (SE) and SP of the intraoperative RGS approach using a β-probe with a per-lesion analysis, while assessing safety and feasibility as secondary objectives. Methods: This prospective, single-arm, single-center, phase II trial ( NCT05448157 ) enrolled 20 patients diagnosed with small intestine NETs (SI-NETs) with positive lesions detected at 68 Ga-DOTA-TOC positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT). Patients received an intravenous injection of 1.1 MBq/Kg of 68Ga-DOTA-TOC 10 min prior to surgery. In vivo measurements were conducted using a β-probe. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed, with the tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) as the independent variable and pathology result (cancer vs. non-cancer) as the dependent variable. The area under the curve (AUC), optimal TBR, and absorbed dose for the surgery staff were reported. Results: The intraoperative RGS approach was feasible in all cases without adverse effects. Of 134 specimens, the AUC was 0.928, with a TBR cut-off of 1.35 yielding 89.3% SE and 86.4% SP. The median absorbed dose for the surgery staff was 30 µSv (range 12-41 µSv). Conclusion: This study reports optimal accuracy in detecting lesions of SI-NETs using the intraoperative RGS approach with a novel β-probe. The method was found to be safe, feasible, and easily reproducible in daily clinical practice, with minimal radiation exposure for the staff. RGS might potentially improve radical resection rates in SI-NETs. PubMed Full Content: Full text links Cite Display options Display options Format Abstract PubMed PMID Abstract Objective: to evaluate the feasibility of the intra-operative application of a specimen PET/CT imager in a clinical setting. Materials and methods: this is a pilot analysis performed in three patients who received an intra-operative administration of 68 Ga-PSMA-11 (n = 2) and 68 Ga-DOTA-TOC (n = 1), respectively. Patients were administrated with PET radiopharmaceuticals to perform radio-guided surgery with a beta-probe detector during radical prostatectomy for prostate cancer (PCa) and salvage lymphadenectomy for recurrent neuroendocrine tumor (NET) of the ileum, respectively. All procedures have been performed within two ongoing clinical trials in our Institute ( NCT05596851 and NCT05448157 ). Pathologic assessment with immunohistochemistry (PSMA-staining and SSA immunoreactivity) was considered as standard of truth. Specimen images were compared with baseline PET/CT images and histopathological analysis. Results: Patients received 1 MBq/Kg of 68 Ga-PSMA-11 (PCa) or 1.2 MBq/Kg of 68 Ga-DOTA-TOC (NET) prior to surgery. Specimens were collected, positioned in the dedicated specimen container, and scanned to obtain high-resolution PET/CT images. In all cases, a perfect match was observed between the findings detected by the specimen imager and histopathology. Overall, the PET spatial resolution was sensibly higher for the specimen images compared to the baseline whole-body PET/CT images. Furthermore, the use of the PET/CT specimen imager did not significantly interfere with any procedures, and the overall length of the surgery was not affected using the PET/CT specimen imager. Finally, the radiation exposure of the operating theater staff was lower than 40 µSv per procedure (range 26-40 μSv). Conclusions: the image acquisition of specimens obtained by patients who received intra-surgery injections of 68 Ga-PSMA-11 and 68 Ga-DOTA-TOC was feasible and reliable also in a live-experience session and has been easily adapted to surgery daily practice. The high sensitivity, together with the evaluation of intra-lesion tumor heterogeneity, were the most relevant results since the data derived from specimen PET/CT imaging matched perfectly with the histopathological analysis.
