# ROLE & GOAL

You are an evidence-based medical oncologist specializing in neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs), including well-differentiated NETs and poorly differentiated NECs. You will receive structured information about one patient and one study with published results. Your job is to identify whether this study provides meaningful clinical evidence that could inform therapy recommendations for this patient.

**CRITICAL: This is NOT about trial enrollment eligibility.** You are evaluating whether the study's published evidence, outcomes, and treatment approach are clinically relevant and could guide therapeutic decision-making for this patient, regardless of strict inclusion/exclusion criteria.

DECISION RULE: When does a study provide meaningful evidence for therapy recommendations?

Answer YES if ALL THREE criteria are met; otherwise answer NO.

	1.	**Disease / Biology Alignment** (should align reasonably)
Core disease features should be reasonably concordant: differentiation (NET vs NEC), primary site/subtype, grade/Ki-67 range, stage (localized vs advanced/metastatic), functional status. The study population should be clinically similar enough that treatment insights are transferable.

	•	"Reasonably concordant" allows clinically justified extrapolation (e.g., pancreatic NET G2 evidence may inform pancreatic NET G1-G2 treatment decisions, midgut NET evidence may apply to small bowel NET with similar characteristics).

	2.	**Treatment Context / Clinical Applicability** (should be applicable in at least one clinical scenario)
The study's treatment approach, setting, and patient population should provide meaningful evidence for at least one relevant clinical scenario for this patient. Consider:
	•	**Current state:** Patient's actual clinical situation
	•	**Alternative scenarios:** e.g. What if surgery is/isn't feasible? What if SSTR-PET is positive? Different treatment sequences?
	•	**Treatment approach:** Is the studied intervention something that could be considered for this patient?
	•	**Eligibility context:** While strict eligibility criteria shouldn't be a hard rejection, consider if the study population is reasonably similar to this patient.
	•	**Evidence transferability:** Can the treatment insights from this study population be reasonably applied to this patient's situation?

	3.	**Evidence Quality and Relevance** (should provide actionable clinical insights)
The published results should offer meaningful evidence that could guide treatment decisions:
	•	Study provides clear efficacy and/or safety data
	•	Results are clinically meaningful (not just statistical significance)
	•	Patient population studied is representative enough to inform decisions for this patient
	•	**Eligibility considerations:** While the patient may not meet all inclusion criteria, the study population should be sufficiently similar that treatment insights are transferable
	•	Evidence strength justifies consideration in treatment planning

**IMPORTANT:** Publication quality is not a standalone criterion. Focus on whether the prospective clinical evidence (even from preliminary results or abstracts) provides meaningful insights for treatment decisions.

If any of the three criteria cannot be verified as met from the provided fields AND reasonable clinical assumptions, respond NO.
Apply clinical judgment when evaluating borderline cases - prioritize studies with strong biological rationale, meaningful clinical outcomes, and evidence that could realistically inform treatment decisions for this patient.

=== ASSESSMENT RESULTS ===
Clinical Assessment:
**Patient Clinical Profile and Disease Status**
The patient is a 68-year-old female with a well-differentiated, Grade 2 (Ki-67 3–5%) functional duodenal neuroendocrine tumor (NET), initially diagnosed in 2013. The disease course is characterized by synchronous liver metastases and a long natural history (12 years). The patient presents with liver-only disease progression (confirmed by RECIST PD in October 2025) following a sequence of extensive multimodal treatments.

**Treatment History and Resistance Profile**
The patient is heavily pre-treated and has demonstrated disease progression on multiple therapeutic lines:
1.  **Somatostatin Analogues (SSA):** Progressed after initial control (started 2013).
2.  **Locoregional and Surgical Therapy:** Underwent TAE, resection of the primary duodenal neoplasm, and liver resection (2013–2014), followed by multiple subsequent locoregional therapies.
3.  **Peptide Receptor Radionuclide Therapy (PRRT):** Administered in 2017–2018 due to liver progression.
4.  **Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors (TKIs):**
    *   **Surufatinib:** Achieved long-term control (>40 cycles) within a phase II trial but eventually progressed.
    *   **Sunitinib:** Initiated in June 2025 for mild hepatic progression; resulted in stable disease initially but failed by October 2025 (RECIST PD).

**Current Clinical Assessment**
Despite radiological progression, the patient maintains an excellent performance status (ECOG PS 0) and is currently asymptomatic ("no relevant clinical symptoms"), suggesting a dissociation between tumor growth and clinical deterioration. The tumor remains well-differentiated with a relatively low proliferative index (Ki-67 3–5%).

**Guideline-Based Assessment of Management Options**
Based on the *ESMO Clinical Practice Guidelines for diagnosis, treatment and follow-up*, the following factors are relevant for the tumor board's assessment:

*   **Systemic Therapy (mTOR Inhibitors):** The patient has progressed on TKIs (Surufatinib and Sunitinib). The guidelines recommend Everolimus for the treatment of advanced, non-functional neuroendocrine tumors of the gastrointestinal tract (RADIANT-4 trial). While the patient's tumor was originally functional, she is currently asymptomatic. The guidelines note that Everolimus should be used in patients with clearly progressive GI NETs.
*   **Chemotherapy:** The guidelines state that results with systemic chemotherapy for advanced well-differentiated G1/G2 GI NETs are poor (ORR 11.5%) and generally "cannot be recommended." Chemotherapy is typically reserved for rapidly progressive tumors or those with higher Ki-67 (e.g., 15–20%). Given this patient's Ki-67 of 3–5% and long disease history, chemotherapy may have limited utility.
*   **Locoregional Therapy:** As the patient presents with liver-only progression, the guidelines suggest that vascular and ablative locoregional modalities (e.g., embolization, ablation) can be considered as an alternative or adjunct to systemic therapy in patients with liver-limited disease.
*   **PRRT Re-treatment Consideration:** The patient received PRRT 7–8 years ago. While the guidelines primarily discuss PRRT as a second-line therapy after SSA failure, they acknowledge it as a therapeutic option in progressive SSTR-positive NETs. Re-evaluation of Somatostatin Receptor (SSTR) status is essential.
    *   *Crucial Constraint:* Eligibility for any further PRRT consideration requires confirmation via [68]Ga/[64]Cu-SSTR-PET-CT that uptake in all metastatic lesions is significantly higher than the liver background. If lesions show low or heterogeneous uptake, PRRT is generally not indicated.
*   **Surufatinib Context:** The guidelines acknowledge recent data (SANET-ep) indicating activity of Surufatinib in extra-pancreatic NETs, consistent with the patient's prior response, though she has now developed resistance to this class.

**Diagnostic Requirements**
To finalize the management plan, updated staging and molecular imaging are necessary to characterize the metabolic activity and receptor status of the progressing hepatic lesions, specifically to distinguish between slow progression suitable for locoregional control versus systemic progression requiring a switch in drug class (e.g., to an mTOR inhibitor).

PubMed Full Content: Full text links Cite Display options Display options Format Abstract PubMed PMID Abstract The prognosis of patients with advanced high-grade (G3) digestive neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) is rather poor. The addition of immune checkpoint inhibition to platinum-based chemotherapy may improve survival. NICE-NEC ( NCT03980925 ) is a single-arm, phase II trial that recruited chemotherapy-naive, unresectable advanced or metastatic G3 NENs of gastroenteropancreatic (GEP) or unknown origin. Patients received nivolumab 360 mg intravenously (iv) on day 1, carboplatin AUC 5 iv on day 1, and etoposide 100 mg/m 2 /d iv on days 1-3, every 3 weeks for up to six cycles, followed by nivolumab 480 mg every 4 weeks for up to 24 months, disease progression, death or unacceptable toxicity. The primary endpoint was the 12-month overall survival (OS) rate (H 0 50%, H 1 72%, β 80%, α 5%). Secondary endpoints were objective response rate (ORR), duration of response (DoR), progression-free survival (PFS), and safety. From 2019 to 2021, 37 patients were enrolled. The most common primary sites were the pancreas (37.8%), stomach (16.2%) and colon (10.8%). Twenty-five patients (67.6%) were poorly differentiated carcinomas (NECs) and/or had a Ki67 index >55%. The ORR was 56.8%. Median PFS was 5.7 months (95%CI: 5.1-9) and median OS 13.9 months (95%CI: 8.3-Not reached), with a 12-month OS rate of 54.1% (95%CI: 40.2-72.8) that did not meet the primary endpoint. However, 37.6% of patients were long-term survivors (>2 years). The safety profile was consistent with previous reports. There was one treatment-related death. Nivolumab plus platinum-based chemotherapy was associated with prolonged survival in over one-third of chemonaïve patients with G3 GEP-NENs, with a manageable safety profile. © 2024. The Author(s).
