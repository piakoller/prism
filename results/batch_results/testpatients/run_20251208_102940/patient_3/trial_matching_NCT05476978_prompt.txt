**Guideline-Based Assessment of Second-Line Options**
According to the ENETS 2023 and ESMO 2020 guidelines, the following considerations apply to the selection of second-line treatment for a patient with progressive NF-Pan-NET G1:

1.  **Targeted Therapies (Everolimus/Sunitinib):**
    *   The ENETS guidelines (Section 3.3, Q6) and ESMO guidelines recommend Everolimus and Sunitinib for patients with progressive advanced Pan-NETs.
    *   These agents are established options for G1/G2 tumors after the failure of somatostatin analogs.
    *   Given the patient's renal impairment, the metabolic clearance profiles of these drugs make them relevant considerations, though careful monitoring of comorbidities (e.g., diabetes with Everolimus) is required.

2.  **Peptide Receptor Radionuclide Therapy (PRRT):**
    *   ENETS (Section 3.2, Q5) and ESMO guidelines list PRRT (specifically 177Lu-DOTATATE) as a valid therapeutic option for progressive Pan-NETs.
    *   **Prerequisites:** Eligibility strictly requires confirmation of strong somatostatin receptor expression via SSTR-PET/CT (e.g., 68Ga-DOTATATE). Uptake must be higher than liver background in all primary tumor lesions and metastases. The patient information does not currently provide SSTR-PET/CT results; this imaging is mandatory before PRRT can be considered.
    *   **Renal Contraindication:** The patient's current creatinine of 2.24 mg/dL poses a significant safety concern. Guidelines warn that renal dysfunction is a potential adverse event of PRRT. The ESMO guidelines note that mild renal toxicity can occur, and ENETS highlights the need to screen for renal dysfunction. This level of renal impairment may contraindicate PRRT or require specific dosimetry and nephrological clearance.

3.  **Chemotherapy:**
    *   ENETS (Section 3.4, Q7) and ESMO guidelines mention chemotherapy (Temozolomide/Capecitabine or Streptozotocin/5-FU) for progressive Pan-NETs, particularly when tumor burden is high.
    *   While the patient has a high tumor burden (bulky primary and >25 liver metastases), the indolent biology (G1, Ki67 2%) and significant renal impairment (Cr 2.24 mg/dL) complicate the use of regimens containing renal-cleared agents like Streptozotocin or Capecitabine.

**Conclusion**
The patient has progressive, high-burden, well-differentiated G1 NF-Pan-NET. While guidelines support Targeted Therapies, PRRT, or Chemotherapy as second-line options, the clinical assessment is heavily influenced by the patient's renal insufficiency (Creatinine 2.24 mg/dL). SSTR-PET/CT imaging is required to determine receptor status; however, even if SSTR-positive, the renal function may limit PRRT eligibility.

PubMed Full Content: Full text links Cite Display options Display options Format Abstract PubMed PMID Abstract Importance: Diagnosing solid lesions in the pancreas via endoscopic ultrasonographic (EUS) images is challenging. Artificial intelligence (AI) has the potential to help with such diagnosis, but existing AI models focus solely on a single modality. Objective: To advance the clinical diagnosis of solid lesions in the pancreas through developing a multimodal AI model integrating both clinical information and EUS images. Design, setting, and participants: In this randomized crossover trial conducted from January 1 to June 30, 2023, from 4 centers across China, 12 endoscopists of varying levels of expertise were randomly assigned to diagnose solid lesions in the pancreas with or without AI assistance. Endoscopic ultrasonographic images and clinical information of 439 patients from 1 institution who had solid lesions in the pancreas between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2022, were collected to train and validate the joint-AI model, while 189 patients from 3 external institutions were used to evaluate the robustness and generalizability of the model. Intervention: Conventional or AI-assisted diagnosis of solid lesions in the pancreas. Main outcomes and measures: In the retrospective dataset, the performance of the joint-AI model was evaluated internally and externally. In the prospective dataset, diagnostic performance of the endoscopists with or without the AI assistance was compared. Results: The retrospective dataset included 628 patients (400 men [63.7%]; mean [SD] age, 57.7 [27.4] years) who underwent EUS procedures. A total of 130 patients (81 men [62.3%]; mean [SD] age, 58.4 [11.7] years) were prospectively recruited for the crossover trial. The area under the curve of the joint-AI model ranged from 0.996 (95% CI, 0.993-0.998) in the internal test dataset to 0.955 (95% CI, 0.940-0.968), 0.924 (95% CI, 0.888-0.955), and 0.976 (95% CI, 0.942-0.995) in the 3 external test datasets, respectively. The diagnostic accuracy of novice endoscopists was significantly enhanced with AI assistance (0.69 [95% CI, 0.61-0.76] vs 0.90 [95% CI, 0.83-0.94]; P < .001), and the supplementary interpretability information alleviated the skepticism of the experienced endoscopists. Conclusions and relevance: In this randomized crossover trial of diagnosing solid lesions in the pancreas with or without AI assistance, the joint-AI model demonstrated positive human-AI interaction, which suggested its potential to facilitate a clinical diagnosis. Nevertheless, future randomized clinical trials are warranted. Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT05476978 .
