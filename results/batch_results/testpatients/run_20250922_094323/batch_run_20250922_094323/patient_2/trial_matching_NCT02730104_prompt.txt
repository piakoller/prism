Clinical Assessment:
### **Clinical Assessment for Tumor Board**

**Patient:** 70-year-old male (ID: 2)
**Diagnosis:** Metastatic, well-differentiated, non-functioning Grade 2 neuroendocrine tumor (NET) of probable pancreatic origin.

**1. Summary of Clinical Presentation**
This is a 70-year-old male with a history of ischemic heart disease who presented with constitutional symptoms, including significant weight loss (7 kg), asthenia, and hyporexia. The diagnostic workup has confirmed a newly diagnosed, widely metastatic, well-differentiated Grade 2 neuroendocrine tumor. The primary is presumed to be in the head of the pancreas based on imaging and immunohistochemistry (IHC) findings from a liver biopsy (TTF1-negative, CDX2-negative).

**2. Histopathological and Staging Details**
*   **Histology:** The liver biopsy confirms a Grade 2 NET with a Ki-67 of 15% and 1.6 mitoses/2 mm². This proliferation index is in the upper range for G2 tumors, which can be associated with more active disease, as noted in the ESMO guidelines.
*   **Staging:** The patient has advanced, Stage IV disease with a high tumor burden. The Ga-68 PET/CT on July 30, 2025, demonstrated widespread disease involving:
    *   **Pancreas:** A 11×10 mm lesion in the pancreatic head.
    *   **Liver:** Multiple hepatic metastases.
    *   **Lung:** A 17 mm nodule in the lower left lobe (LLL).
    *   **Bone:** Multiple bone metastases.
*   **Clinical Status:** The patient is symptomatic from his disease, which is a critical factor in determining the urgency and type of first-line therapy.

**3. Analysis of Somatostatin Receptor (SSTR) Imaging**
The Ga-68 PET/CT confirms SSTR expression in all known tumor sites. However, the uptake is heterogeneous:
*   **High Avidity:** Bone metastases (SUV 12) and hepatic metastases (SUV 8-9) show high SSTR avidity.
*   **Moderate Avidity:** The LLL lung nodule shows moderate uptake (SUV 6).
*   **Low Avidity:** The presumed primary lesion in the pancreatic head demonstrates low SSTR avidity (SUV 3). This uptake is significantly lower than in the metastatic sites and is likely below the background uptake of normal liver tissue. This finding has important implications for therapies targeting SSTR, such as PRRT. The ESMO and ENETS guidelines emphasize that PRRT is most effective in tumors with homogenous and high SSTR expression.

**4. Guideline-Based Therapeutic Considerations**
The primary question is the most appropriate first-line systemic therapy for this symptomatic patient with high-burden, metastatic G2 Pan-NET.

*   **Chemotherapy:** The ENETS 2023 guidelines (Section 3.4, Q7) state that patient selection for chemotherapy relies on features such as bulky, symptomatic disease due to high volume, or significant tumor growth. They recommend temozolomide with capecitabine for metastatic progressive and/or symptomatic G1-G2 NF-Pan-NETs (Level of evidence 2b). Similarly, the ESMO 2020 guidelines (Figure 4) recommend chemotherapy (e.g., CAPTEM) as a primary option for Pan-NETs with rapid growth, high tumor burden, and symptoms. This patient's clinical profile aligns directly with these indications.

*   **Peptide Receptor Radionuclide Therapy (PRRT):** While the ENETS guidelines (Figure 1) list PRRT as a potential first-line option for high-burden disease, they also recommend it as a second-line treatment (Level of evidence 2b). The ESMO guidelines position PRRT as a later-line option for Pan-NETs, to be used after failure of other approved therapies [III, A]. A critical limiting factor for upfront PRRT in this case is the low SSTR uptake (SUV 3) in the primary pancreatic tumor, which suggests that this lesion may not respond adequately to PRRT and raises concerns about treatment efficacy.

*   **Somatostatin Analogues (SSAs):** According to both ENETS (Section 3.3.1, Q6) and ESMO guidelines, SSA monotherapy is the recommended upfront treatment for *slow-growing*, SSTR-positive, advanced G1 and G2 NF-Pan-NETs, preferably with a Ki-67 <10%. Given this patient's high tumor burden, significant symptoms, and Ki-67 of 15%, SSAs as a single agent are unlikely to provide sufficient initial antiproliferative effect or symptom control related to tumor mass.

*   **Targeted Therapies (Everolimus/Sunitinib):** The ENETS and ESMO guidelines recommend everolimus and sunitinib for *progressive* G1 and G2 Pan-NETs [Level of evidence 1a]. While this patient has newly diagnosed disease, the high burden and symptoms imply it is active and progressive. These agents are a valid consideration. However, the ENETS guidelines (Section 3.4, Q7) note that chemotherapy may be favored when the goal is tumor debulking to reduce the volume of disease, which is a relevant goal in this symptomatic, high-burden patient.

**5. Patient-Specific Factors**
The patient's age (70) and history of ischemic heart disease with stent placement are important considerations. The potential toxicities of any systemic therapy, including chemotherapy (myelosuppression), targeted agents (stomatitis, metabolic effects, cardiac events), and PRRT (renal and hematologic toxicity), must be carefully weighed against his comorbidities and performance status.

**Conclusion of Assessment**
This patient presents with a symptomatic, high-volume, metastatic Grade 2 Pan-NET with a Ki-67 of 15%. The clinical scenario calls for a first-line systemic therapy capable of inducing a response to control tumor growth and alleviate symptoms. Based on the provided ENETS and ESMO guidelines, the patient's presentation (high tumor burden, symptoms, Ki-67 15%) strongly aligns with the indications for upfront chemotherapy. The low SSTR-avidity of the primary pancreatic lesion makes PRRT a less suitable first-line choice. SSA monotherapy is likely insufficient for initial disease control in this context.

This non-interventional, prospective, observational study evaluated the real-world safety and effectiveness of lanreotide depot, a somatostatin analog (SSA) used to treat GEP-NETs, in a community setting. Methods: In this prospective, non-interventional study ( NCT02730104 ), adult patients with locally advanced (inoperable), metastatic GEP-NETs treated with lanreotide depot were evaluated by their physician every 6 months from enrollment for 24 months. Clinically defined time to disease progression (TTDP) and overall survival (OS) were estimated for the total population and by primary tumor type (gastrointestinal [GI], pancreatic, unknown origin), and an exploratory analysis determined the rate of progression-free survival (PFS) at 12 and 24 months. Patient satisfaction was evaluated via the Treatment Satisfaction Questionnaire for Medication (TSQM-9), and safety information was recorded. Results: Of 99 patients, the 24-month PFS rate was 73.7% (95% confidence interval [CI] 63.1-81.7) and 24-month OS rate was 84.2% (95% CI 74.0-90.7). Median TTDP was not reached because few patients experienced disease progression during the study period. The majority of responding patients expressed satisfaction with treatment on each domain of the TSQM-9. Treatment-related adverse events (AEs) occurred in 19.2% of patients, while no serious AEs (SAEs) were related to the study drug. Conclusions: Lanreotide depot is an effective and well-tolerated treatment for GEP-NETs in the real-world community setting. Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier, NCT02730104 . Plain language summary Gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (GEP-NETs) are rare tumors that develop in the gut or pancreas. GEP-NETs may lead to a reduced lifespan, and people with GEP-NETs may have symptoms such as fatigue, muscle weakness, diarrhea, stomach pain/cramping, and skin reactions. One approved treatment for patients whose tumor cannot be removed with surgery is lanreotide depot. Clinical trials have found lanreotide depot to be effective at prolonging survival and managing symptoms in people with GEP-NETs. However, clinical trials take place under very strict conditions and often do not represent all people with a certain disease in the ‘real world’. It is important to determine whether treatments are still effective when used outside of clinical trials. This study was conducted in the real world and followed 99 people with GEP-NETs whose physicians were treating them with lanreotide depot. Each person was monitored for 24 months and assessed during check-ups by their physician every 6 months. After 24 months, 73.7% of people did not have progression (worsening) of disease. The percentage of people who had not died by the end of the study was 84.2%. Most patients (91.6%) said they were satisfied with their treatment. Only 19.2% of patients experienced side effects, none of which were serious. © 2022. The Author(s).
