Clinical Assessment:
### Clinical Assessment for Tumor Board

**Patient Summary:**
This is a 78-year-old male with a well-differentiated, nonfunctioning pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor (NF-Pan-NET), grade 1 (Ki-67 2%), with a large primary mass in the pancreatic uncinate process (90x49 mm) and extensive liver metastases (at least 25 lesions). He has been treated with first-line lanreotide autogel for over four years and now demonstrates clear radiological evidence of disease progression. The patient's performance status is good (ECOG 0–1) and he is asymptomatic from a tumor perspective. Significant comorbidities include type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease, with recent worsening of renal function (creatinine 2.24 mg/dL from a baseline of 1.6–1.8 mg/dL).

**Analysis of Disease Status:**
- **Diagnosis:** The diagnosis of a well-differentiated G1 NF-Pan-NET is histologically confirmed (liver biopsy), consistent with the IHC profile (chromogranin A+, synaptophysin+). This is typically an indolent tumor, but the patient presents with a high tumor burden.
- **Progression:** The patient meets the criteria for disease progression on a somatostatin analogue (SSA), as defined by an increase in the size of both the primary pancreatic mass and multiple liver metastases on the most recent MRI. According to both the ENETS 2023 and ESMO 2020 guidelines, this progression warrants consideration of second-line systemic therapy.

**Evaluation of Second-Line Systemic Treatment Options based on Guidelines:**

The primary clinical question is the selection of the most appropriate second-line systemic therapy. The main options for progressive, advanced NF-Pan-NETs, as outlined in the provided ENETS 2023 and ESMO 2020 guidelines, are peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT), molecular targeted therapies (everolimus, sunitinib), and systemic chemotherapy.

1.  **Peptide Receptor Radionuclide Therapy (PRRT):**
    - **Guideline Context:** PRRT is a recommended second-line treatment for progressive, SSTR-positive G1-G2 NF-Pan-NETs (ENETS 2023, Level 2b, Grade B; ESMO 2020, Level III, A).
    - **Patient-Specific Considerations:**
        - A critical piece of information is missing: there is no mention of a somatostatin receptor imaging (SRI) scan, such as a 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT. This imaging is mandatory to confirm sufficient and homogeneous SSTR expression, which is a prerequisite for PRRT eligibility.
        - The patient's worsening renal function (creatinine 2.24 mg/dL) poses a significant relative contraindication. Both guidelines acknowledge the risk of iatrogenic adverse events, including renal dysfunction, with PRRT (ENETS 2023, Section 4.1; ESMO 2020, Section on PRRT). The existing chronic kidney disease significantly increases the risk of further, potentially irreversible, nephrotoxicity.

2.  **Molecular Targeted Therapies (Everolimus and Sunitinib):**
    - **Guideline Context:** Both everolimus and sunitinib are strongly recommended for progressive G1-G2 NF-Pan-NETs based on Level 1a evidence (ENETS 2023, Grade A; ESMO 2020, Level I, A).
    - **Patient-Specific Considerations:**
        - **Everolimus:** The RADIANT-3 trial demonstrated a significant PFS benefit. However, a known side effect is hyperglycemia (ESMO 2020). Given the patient's history of type 2 diabetes, initiating everolimus would require close glycemic monitoring and potential adjustment of his oral antidiabetic medications.
        - **Sunitinib:** The pivotal phase 3 trial also showed a significant PFS improvement. A common adverse event is hypertension (ESMO 2020). The patient's pre-existing hypertension would need to be carefully managed. Sunitinib is also associated with diarrhea, which could exacerbate his current grade 1 toxicity.
        - The primary goal of these agents is typically disease stabilization, as objective response rates are modest (<10%) (ESMO 2020).

3.  **Systemic Chemotherapy:**
    - **Guideline Context:** Chemotherapy is a key treatment modality for advanced Pan-NETs. The ENETS 2023 guidelines suggest temozolomide with capecitabine (CAPTEM) or streptozotocin (STZ) + 5-FU as options for progressive G1-G2 NF-Pan-NETs (Level 2b, Grade B). ESMO 2020 also recommends these regimens (Level II, A/B).
    - **Patient-Specific Considerations:**
        - **Streptozotocin (STZ)-based regimens:** STZ is known for its significant renal toxicity (ENETS 2023, Section 4.1). Given the patient's elevated creatinine of 2.24 mg/dL, STZ-based chemotherapy would carry a high risk of inducing or accelerating renal failure and should be avoided.
        - **Temozolomide and Capecitabine (CAPTEM):** This regimen has a more favorable renal safety profile. The ECOG-ACRIN E2211 trial demonstrated a superior PFS for CAPTEM over temozolomide alone and a notable objective response rate (40% per ENETS 2023 summary). The ENETS guidelines note that chemotherapy is a consideration for patients with bulky disease or when tumor shrinkage is a goal (Section 3.4). Given this patient's high tumor burden, the potential for a volumetric response with CAPTEM is a significant clinical advantage over the primarily cytostatic effect of targeted agents.

**Conclusion of Assessment:**
The patient has high-burden, progressive metastatic G1 NF-Pan-NET requiring second-line therapy. His good performance status allows for various treatment options. However, his worsening renal impairment is a major limiting factor for certain therapies. PRRT is not currently a viable option due to the lack of required SSTR imaging and the high risk of nephrotoxicity. STZ-based chemotherapy is similarly contraindicated due to renal risk. The choice lies between targeted agents (everolimus/sunitinib) and the CAPTEM chemotherapy regimen. While targeted agents are well-supported by evidence, they would require careful management of the patient's comorbidities (diabetes and hypertension) and primarily offer disease stabilization. The CAPTEM regimen offers a higher probability of objective tumor response, which may be beneficial in the setting of high tumor burden, and has a more favorable safety profile with respect to the patient's key comorbidity of chronic kidney disease.

	•	Key findings from publications: Onclive Article (Dr Strosberg on 212Pb-DOTAMTATE in Unresectable/Metastatic …): Jonathan Strosberg, MD, professor, Moffitt Cancer Center, discusses findings from the phase 2 ALPHAMEDIX02 trial (NCT05153772) evaluating the safety, tolerability, and efficacy of 212 Pb-DOTAMTATE in patients with somatostatin receptor (SSTR)–positive gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (GEP-NETs). This study included 2 cohorts: patients naive to peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT; cohort 1; n = 36) and patients refractory to PRRT (cohort 2; n = 30). All patients needed to have histologically confirmed, unresectable or metastatic, SSTR-positive GEP-NETs with at least 1 site of measurable disease per RECIST 1.1 criteria. Findings from cohort 1 presented at the 2024 ASCO Annual Meeting showed that evaluable patients not exposed to prior PRRT who received 212Pb-DOTAMTATE in the phase 1 ALPHAMEDIX01 trial (NCT03466216) or ALPHAMEDIX02 (n = 44) achieved an overall response rate (ORR) of 56.8% (95% CI, 42.2%-70.3%), which Strosberg notes is higher than ORRs typically observed in prior clinical trials investigating lutetium Lu 177 dotatate (Lutathera) in this patient population. In the phase 2 portion (n = 36), the ORR was 55.6% (95% CI, 39.6%-70.5%). Regarding safety, the targeted alpha therapy was generally tolerable, Strosberg explains. Notably, the incidences of hematologic and renal toxicities—common concerns with PRRT—were low for patients treated with 212Pb-DOTAMTATE, he continues. Any-grade alopecia was common, occurring in 93% of patients treated in phase 1 or 2 (n = 44), and Strosberg notes that this adverse effect (AE) was usually moderate and reversible. During the presentation at the ASCO Annual Meeting, he explained that SSTR is expressed on hair follicles. One AE that has stood out with 212Pb-DOTAMTATE is dysphagia, Strosberg continues. In evaluable patients treated during ALPHAMEDIX02 (n = 36), the rate of any-grade dysphagia was 42%, and grade 3 or 4 dysphagia was reported in 1 patient (3%). Despite normal colonoscopy findings, esophageal manometry indicated results consistent with achalasia, according to Strosberg, who adds that this AE was generally managed with palliative treatments such as Botox injections.
